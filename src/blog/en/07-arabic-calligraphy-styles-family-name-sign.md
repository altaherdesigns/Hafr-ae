A family name written in Arabic calligraphy is one of the most personal signs you can put on a villa. The style you choose says something about the house: formal or relaxed, traditional or modern, ornate or restrained. The trouble is that the same name can look entirely different in each script, and not every script is easy to cut in metal.

This guide describes the main styles, what each suits, and the practical points that decide whether a design will survive being cut.

## The main styles at a glance

**Thuluth.** A graceful, curved, monumental script, often seen in large inscriptions on mosques and architectural panels. It has strong verticals and sweeping horizontals, with a sense of balance and grandeur. It suits a prominent entrance sign and works at large sizes. It takes skill to draw well, so quality of the artwork matters a lot.

**Naskh.** A clear, regular and highly legible script, commonly used in printed books and Quran copies. It is easy to read at a glance, which makes it practical for names that visitors must recognise quickly. It looks calm and traditional. The risk is that it can look plain unless the proportions are carefully drawn.

**Diwani.** A flowing, compact and decorative script developed for official documents in the Ottoman era. Letters connect and tuck into one another in a rhythmic composition. It looks graceful and distinctive, but it is harder to read and harder to cut, because the letters overlap and the strokes are thin in places. It suits a short name with a strong visual silhouette.

**Kufic.** One of the oldest scripts, angular and geometric. Square Kufic builds letters from straight lines and right angles, and is very well suited to modern architecture and to cutting, because the forms are simple and robust. It can look contemporary and architectural. Legibility varies, so check the name reads correctly for the audience.

**Ruq'ah.** A simple everyday handwriting style with short strokes and little ornament. It looks casual and friendly. It is not the usual choice for a formal entrance sign, but it can suit a modern, relaxed look.

Other styles such as Nastaliq and Maghrebi exist and can be used, but they need an experienced calligrapher and extra attention to cutting.

## Choosing a style for your villa

Ask three questions:

1. **Who must read it?** If visitors and delivery drivers need to read the name quickly, choose a clear script like Naskh, or a simplified Thuluth.
2. **What is the architecture?** Modern, geometric facades pair naturally with square Kufic. Classical or heritage villas suit Thuluth or Naskh.
3. **How long is the name?** A short name can carry a dense, ornate style. A long name often needs a simpler script.

## What changes when letters are cut from metal

Calligraphy was designed for pen and ink. Metal cutting has its own rules, and these affect the result more than most people expect.

![Two plates with a ring-shaped slot cut through them. In the first, the slot is closed all the way round, so the disc in the middle drops out. In the second, two small bridges cross the slot and hold the disc in place](figures/bridges.svg "A closed loop cut from one plate: without bridges the centre falls out; two small bridges hold it in place.")

**Counters and islands.** Many Arabic letters contain closed shapes such as the loops in waw, meem, fa and qaf. If you cut the outline of a closed loop from a single plate, the centre becomes a loose piece. It needs bridges, small connections that hold it in place, or the letter must be redrawn. A skilled designer does this so the bridges look like part of the design.

**Minimum stroke width.** Very thin strokes are fragile, hard to cut cleanly and can bend. Thin hairlines common in Diwani or Nastaliq may need thickening for metal.

**Overlapping letters.** Where one letter passes over another, the design must show them as joined or separated. In a cut sign, overlap often needs to be resolved so the pieces are one shape.

**Dots and diacritics.** Dots are small separate pieces. Decide whether they are cut openings, raised pieces or bridges. Without a plan, the dots may fall out or float.

**Sharp inner corners.** Lasers and CNC routers leave a small radius in inner corners. Design with that in mind.

None of these are reasons to avoid calligraphy. They are reasons to work with someone who has cut Arabic before and who will show you a proof. See [how to tell laser-cut from printed signs](/blog/laser-cut-vs-printed-metal-signs/) for what a good cut should look like.

## The proofing process

For a custom calligraphy sign, expect this sequence:

1. You give the exact spelling of the name or phrase, in writing, with the vowel marks if you need them.
2. A calligrapher or designer draws it in the chosen style.
3. The drawing is converted into a cut path with bridges.
4. You see a proof at actual size, ideally on the wall photo.
5. The spelling is checked by a second person who reads Arabic.
6. Only after your approval is the sign cut.

Do not skip step 5. A misspelled name in metal is expensive to fix.

## A word on religious text

Verses of the Quran, hadith and names of God carry particular responsibility. They should be taken from a verified source, written by a qualified calligrapher or checked against an authoritative text, and reviewed for accuracy before cutting. Never use an unverified image from the internet or a machine-generated design for religious text. A mistake is not only embarrassing but disrespectful.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which Arabic calligraphy is best for a villa name sign?**
Thuluth and Naskh are the classic choices for readability and dignity. Square Kufic suits modern architecture. Choose by who must read it and the style of the house.

**Can any calligraphy be cut in metal?**
Most can, but the design usually needs adjustments such as bridges and thicker strokes. A good workshop adapts the artwork and shows you a proof.

**Can I use my own handwriting?**
Yes. A signature or handwritten name can be traced and prepared for cutting, though thin strokes may need thickening.
