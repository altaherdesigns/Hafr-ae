Two stainless steel signs can look identical on the day they are fitted and very different a year later. The difference is usually the grade. Most quotes say "stainless steel" and stop there, but 304 and 316 behave differently in salt air, and the UAE has a lot of coastline.

This guide explains what separates the two grades, what tea staining is, how far from the sea the difference matters, and how to check the grade you are actually being sold.

## What is the difference between 304 and 316?

Both are austenitic stainless steels, the common family used for architecture, kitchens and signs. They look the same, cut the same and polish the same. The difference is in the recipe.

| | Grade 304 | Grade 316 |
|---|---|---|
| Chromium | About 18% | About 16 to 18% |
| Nickel | About 8% | About 10 to 14% |
| Molybdenum | None | 2 to 3% |
| European number | 1.4301 | 1.4401 (316L is 1.4404) |
| Salt resistance | Good inland | Much better near the sea |
| Cost | Lower | Higher |

That 2 to 3% of molybdenum is what matters. It makes the protective surface layer of the steel much better at resisting chlorides, the salts carried in sea air and spray. Engineers compare grades with a pitting resistance number (PREN). It is roughly 19 for 304 and roughly 24 to 26 for 316: higher means more resistant to pitting. You may also see 316L, a low-carbon version: for a sign it behaves the same as 316.

## Why salt air changes the answer

Stainless steel does not rust like plain steel, because chromium forms a thin, invisible layer on its surface that repairs itself. Chlorides attack that layer. Where salt settles on a surface and stays damp, as it does overnight in UAE humidity, it can break through in small spots.

Inland, away from salt, 304 does very well and you will rarely see a problem. Close to the sea, 304 is the grade most likely to show marks. A common rule of thumb from UK stockists is to use 316 within about 5 km of the coast. Treat that as a guide, not a line on a map. Wind direction, shelter and how often rain washes the surface all change the real exposure.

## What tea staining is, and what it is not

Tea staining is a light brown discolouration on the surface of stainless steel, most often near the coast. It looks like rust, which worries owners, but it is a surface mark rather than structural damage. It can usually be cleaned off.

It is still the last thing you want on a sign that greets every visitor. The Australian Stainless Steel Development Association lists what reduces it:

- **The right grade.** 316 resists it far better than 304.
- **A smooth finish.** Their target is a surface roughness of about 0.5 micrometres (Ra) or smoother. A finely brushed or polished face holds less salt than a coarse one.
- **No contamination.** Particles of ordinary steel from grinding wheels or tools turn into brown spots once they get wet.
- **Good drainage.** Ledges and crevices that hold water and dust stain first.
- **Regular washing.** Plain water and a mild detergent, rinsed off. Avoid household cleaners with bleach or chlorides.

## Which grade should you choose?

- **On the coast or in a beachfront community:** 316, without question.
- **A few kilometres inland, with salty winds:** 316 is the safer choice.
- **Well inland, sheltered and washed by occasional rain:** 304 will usually do well.
- **Not sure:** choose 316. The price difference on a single sign is small compared with replacing it.

## How to check the grade you are quoted

The two grades look identical, so you have to ask. A magnet is not a reliable test: both are mostly non-magnetic, and either can become slightly magnetic where it has been bent or cut.

1. **Ask for the grade in writing on the quote.** "Stainless steel" alone is not enough. It should say 316 or 304.
2. **Ask for the mill certificate.** Sheet from a steel mill normally comes with an inspection certificate, often to EN 10204 type 3.1, which lists the actual chemical composition of that batch. Ask to see the one for your sheet. A 316 certificate shows molybdenum of about 2% or more.
3. **Ask about the finish.** A brushed or polished face, and how the cut edges are finished, matters as much as the grade near the sea.
4. **For a big order, test it.** A molybdenum spot test, a drop of reagent that darkens on 316, can separate the two grades on a hidden spot.

If a supplier hesitates over any of these, treat that as your answer. The [guide to laser-cut and printed signs](/blog/laser-cut-vs-printed-metal-signs/) has more questions worth asking before you order.

## Does the finish matter as much as the grade?

Near the coast, yes. A coarse finish has tiny grooves that hold salt and moisture. A finer brushed or polished finish sheds them, and is easier to wipe clean. Brush lines that run vertically on a sign also drain better than horizontal ones. When you compare quotes, compare the finish as well as the grade. ## Frequently asked questions

**Is 316 stainless steel worth it for an inland villa?**
Often yes, if salty winds reach the area or you want the least upkeep. Well inland and sheltered, 304 is usually fine.

**Can 316 stainless steel stain?**
It is far more resistant, but no stainless is completely immune. A smooth finish and an occasional wash with fresh water keep it clean.

**How can I tell 304 and 316 apart?**
Not by eye or with a magnet. Ask for the grade in writing and for the mill certificate, or have a molybdenum spot test done.

**Is "marine grade" the same as 316?**
It is a common sales name for 316, but it is not a standard. Ask for the grade number in writing.
