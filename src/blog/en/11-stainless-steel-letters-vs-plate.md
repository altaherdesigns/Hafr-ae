Once you know what your sign will say, there is one more decision that changes how it looks: whether the name is cut into a single plate, or cut as separate letters fixed straight onto the wall. Both can be made from the same stainless steel, in the same font, at the same size. They still give a very different result.

This guide compares the two on look, fixing, wall surface, cleaning, lighting and cost, so you can choose the one that suits your wall.

## The two options

- **A plate.** One piece of metal carries the whole name. The letters are either cut through the plate, so the wall or a light shows behind them, or fixed onto its face. The plate is fixed to the wall at a few points.
- **Individual letters.** Each letter is cut as its own piece and fixed to the wall separately, usually on pins set into drilled holes. There is no background panel: the wall itself is the background.

## How each one looks

A plate frames the name. It gives a defined edge and a background colour or finish of its own, so the name reads clearly even on a busy wall. It suits a pier, a gate, or any surface with texture, joints or other fittings nearby.

Individual letters look lighter and more architectural. Each one casts its own shadow, and the wall shows between them, so the name seems to sit on the building rather than on a sign. They suit a large, plain, smooth wall where nothing competes with them, and they work well on modern facades.

| | Plate | Individual letters |
|---|---|---|
| Look | Framed, defined, reads on any wall | Light, architectural, part of the wall |
| Best wall | Pier, gate, textured or uneven surface | Large, smooth, plain wall |
| Fixing | A few fixings, one template | Fixings for every letter, careful spacing |
| Alignment | Set in the workshop | Set on site, letter by letter |
| Cleaning | One surface to wipe | Each letter, plus the wall between |
| Fine scripts | Handles thin strokes and dots | Small pieces need extra care |
| Lighting | Halo around the plate or through cut letters | Halo behind each letter |

## The wall decides more than taste

Look closely at the surface before you choose.

- **Smooth render or stone in good condition** takes individual letters well, because each one sits at the same distance from a flat surface.
- **Rough stone, uneven render or a joint pattern** makes individual letters look uneven, because each one follows the bumps. A plate bridges them.
- **Cladding panels and tiles** need care with either option. Fixing positions must avoid joints and whatever sits behind the panel.
- **A narrow pier** rarely has room for letters to breathe. A plate is usually the better fit.

## Fixing and alignment

A plate is aligned once, in the workshop. On site it needs a few holes, usually from a paper template, and it is level or it is not. Fixing letters takes more work and more skill. Every letter needs its own holes in the right place, spacing between letters must be set by eye, and the baseline must stay straight across the wall. A good fitter uses a full-size template for the whole name.

If the sign may ever need to come down, for repainting the wall or for a move, a plate is also much easier to remove and refit.

## Arabic names and fine scripts

Arabic calligraphy has dots, thin strokes and letters that join. On a plate these all stay part of one piece, which is strong and easy to fix. As separate letters, small parts such as dots become tiny loose pieces that each need their own fixing, and thin strokes can bend. Many Arabic names are therefore cut as one connected piece rather than letter by letter, or set on a plate. ## Lighting each option

Both can be lit from behind, which gives a halo of light on the wall.

- **A plate** can glow around its edge, or through letters cut out of it.
- **Individual letters** each throw their own halo, which looks striking on a plain, light-coloured wall.

Sign makers often quote a gap of about 38 mm between the back of a halo-lit letter and the wall, and the gap affects how far the glow spreads. A matte wall finish shows the halo better than a dark glossy one, which can reflect the LEDs as bright spots. ## What drives the cost

Neither option is always cheaper. What moves the price:

- **Number of pieces.** Every separate letter is cut, finished and fixed on its own, so a long name made as letters costs more in labour.
- **Fixings.** A few fixings for a plate, or several for every letter.
- **Size of the background.** A plate uses more metal behind the name.
- **Lighting.** Lighting each letter separately is more involved than lighting one plate.
- **Installation.** Letters take longer to set out and fix on site.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do individual letters look better than a plate?**
On a large, smooth, plain wall they often do. On a pier, a gate or a textured wall, a plate usually looks neater.

**Can I fix stainless steel letters myself?**
Small, light letters on a flat wall, yes, with a full-size template. For heavy, lit or high letters, use a fitter.

**Which is easier to keep clean?**
A plate, because there is one surface to wipe. Letters collect dust on their top edges and on the wall behind them.

**Can Arabic calligraphy be made as separate letters?**
Sometimes, but many Arabic names are cut as one connected piece or set on a plate, because of the joins and the small dots.
