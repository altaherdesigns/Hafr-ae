Many villa owners in the UAE want their family name in both Arabic and English. Arabic for the family, the neighbours and the heritage of the name; English for visitors, drivers and anyone who doesn't read Arabic. Putting both on one sign is common, but it is also where layouts most often go wrong: two scripts fighting for attention, mismatched sizes, or an English spelling that differs from the one on the family's documents.

This guide covers the decisions in order: which script leads, how to size them, how to arrange them, how to spell the English, which digits to use and how to pair the fonts.

## Decide which script leads

A bilingual sign works best when one script is clearly the main one and the other supports it. Two scripts at equal weight compete, and the sign reads as a label rather than a name.

On a family-name sign, Arabic usually leads. It is the original form of the name, and calligraphy gives it presence. English sits below it or beside it, smaller and simpler. There are good reasons to reverse that: a villa whose visitors are mostly non-Arabic readers, or a sign where the house number matters more than the name. Decide on purpose rather than by default.

## Size by eye, not by numbers

Arabic and Latin letters are built differently. Arabic letters have a smaller body relative to their tall and low strokes, so at the same type size Arabic text usually looks smaller than English. If you set both at the same size on a drawing, the Arabic will look weaker than you intended.

Instead:

1. Set the leading script at the size your wall and viewing distance need.
2. Set the second script noticeably smaller, so the hierarchy is obvious.
3. Compare them on a full-size printout on the wall, and adjust until the balance looks right from the road.

## Stacked or side by side?

| Arrangement | How it works | Suits | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Arabic above English | Two lines on one centre line | Most piers and plates | Keep a clear gap between the lines |
| English above Arabic | The same, reversed | Signs where English must read first | Arabic can look like a footnote |
| Side by side | Arabic on the right, English on the left | Wide, low walls | A divider or gap keeps them apart |
| Arabic only, number in both | Name in calligraphy, number readable by all | Heritage designs | Visitors who don't read Arabic rely on the number |

Side by side, Arabic normally goes on the right, because it reads from right to left and the eye meets it first. Stacked, centre both lines on the same axis. A left-aligned English line under a centred Arabic line looks unfinished. Our [design ideas guide](/blog/villa-name-plate-design-ideas/) shows more layouts.

## Spell the English name once, and keep it

Arabic names have no single official English spelling. Al and El are both common, and many names can be written in several ways. The rule is simple: use the spelling the family already uses on passports, Emirates ID and company documents. Write it out for the sign maker yourself rather than letting them transliterate.

Then check the details:

- Is "Al" joined with a hyphen, a space, or nothing?
- Is it capitalised as "Al" or "al"?
- Does the sign use the full family name or a shortened form?

Whatever you choose, use the same form on the sign, the number plate and anything else at the entrance.

## Choose your digits

The house number can be written in Western digits, the kind used in English, or in the Eastern Arabic digits often seen in Arabic text. Both are correct. The question is who must read them.

- **Western digits** are read by everyone, including drivers, couriers and visitors from abroad.
- **Eastern Arabic digits** suit a sign that is entirely in Arabic and designed as calligraphy.
- **Both,** one under the other, work on a formal sign with plenty of room.

One useful detail: numbers read from left to right even inside Arabic text, so a number like 24 reads the same way on both lines of a bilingual sign. For size and contrast, see our [house number guide](/blog/house-number-size-visibility/).

## Pair the two fonts

The Arabic and English should look as if they belong to the same sign. Match their character rather than trying to match their shapes.

- **Thuluth or Naskh** pairs well with a classic serif, with contrast between thick and thin strokes.
- **Square Kufic** pairs well with a geometric sans serif, with even strokes and straight lines.
- **Similar stroke weight** matters more than style. A heavy Arabic line beside a thin English one always looks unbalanced.
- **One English typeface only.** Use weight or size for contrast, not a second font.

The [calligraphy styles guide](/blog/arabic-calligraphy-styles-family-name-sign/) describes each Arabic style in more detail.

## Frequently asked questions

**Should Arabic be above or below English on a villa sign?**
Usually above, because it is normally the main script on a family-name sign. Put English first only if most visitors read English.

**Why does the Arabic look smaller than the English?**
Arabic letters have a smaller body relative to their tall strokes. Set the sizes by eye on a full-size proof, not by matching type sizes.

**Which numbers should I use?**
Western digits are read by everyone. Eastern Arabic digits suit an all-Arabic calligraphic sign, and some signs show both.
